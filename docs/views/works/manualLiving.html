Manual Living is a series of three domestic structures designed as small interruptions to routine.
Each changes how the body balances, how furniture responds, or how people move through space, making familiar moments at home more noticeable.

Project Notes

Manual Living began with my experience of growing up in concrete apartment buildings in South Korea. I have always spent much of my time at home, yet when I looked back, much of that time was difficult to recall. Even when I moved, I encountered similar residential environments, from the buildings themselves to familiar layouts, smooth surfaces, bright interiors and ways of using furniture. Over time, I came to feel that everyday actions at home easily became routine.


One object remained unusually distinct in my memory: a wooden rocking chair. I remember its movement, the feeling of wood against my arms, and the creaking sound it made. Nothing extraordinary happened there, but these small physical sensations made those moments feel more present.

This became the starting point for Manual Living: three domestic structures that introduce small interruptions into familiar actions. Balance Seat keeps the body actively involved in balancing. Shift Bench appears stable at first, but when the user leans forward while seated and shifts their weight, the bench tilts forward with them, briefly interrupting the expectation that furniture should remain still. Threshold Fragment interrupts familiar routes through the home, altering how a person moves, pauses or leans within a space.

I wanted this interruption of predictability to continue into the making process itself. Concrete comes directly from the material language of the apartment environments I grew up in, but I did not want to reproduce the precision and repetition associated with standardised construction. Rather than fully casting the forms in moulds, I used moulds selectively and built up or refined other sections by hand. Newspaper and potassium silicate introduced another kind of uncertainty: as the surface dries, it hardens, shrinks and changes, producing fibrous textures that cannot be fully predetermined. Instead of correcting these variations, I allowed them to remain as visible traces of the material and the making process.

Manual Living is not about rejecting the comfort of home, but about bringing moments that have become almost invisible through familiarity back into awareness. That shift from automatic to manual is what I mean by Manual Living.
